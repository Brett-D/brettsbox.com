Homelab

One quiet little PC in the corner runs my firewall, photo library, file share and home automation. Here's what's on it and why.

Scroll

Why self-host?

Partly for privacy: our family photos are ours, and I'd rather they lived on hardware I can touch. Partly because it's the best way I know to really learn how networks, storage and services fit together. And partly because it's genuinely fun to run your own little corner of the internet.

The box

Hardware
Lenovo ThinkCentre Tiny (M920q), 6 cores, 32 GB RAM
Hypervisor
Proxmox VE, running a mix of virtual machines and lightweight containers
Storage
ZFS on four hard drives in two mirrored pairs, about 8 TB usable. More below
Network
OPNsense firewall VM with separate, segmented networks, and an Intel X710 10 GbE card

Inside the box

A ThinkCentre Tiny has no room for 3.5-inch hard drives, so they live in a four-bay hot-swap cage underneath it, with their own power supply behind the cage. Everything sits in a case I designed and printed for it.

System drive
512 GB M.2 NVMe SSD for Proxmox itself, also on ZFS
Big pair
2 × Seagate Exos 7E8 6 TB: 7200 rpm enterprise drives built to run 24/7
Small pair
2 × Seagate Barracuda 2 TB, 7200 rpm desktop drives
Pool
One ZFS pool made of the two mirrors: about 8 TB usable, half of it used
SATA adapter
RIITOP M.2 to SATA: an ASMedia ASM1166 chip, six SATA ports from two PCIe 3.0 lanes
Drive cage
Rosewill RSV-SATA-Cage-34: four hot-swap 3.5-inch trays and a 120 mm fan
Drive power
ESGAMING 300 W Flex-ATX supply, fully modular, with a switch on its 24-pin socket
10 GbE
Intel X710 network card on the ThinkCentre's PCIe riser
Case
3D-printed in two parts, 187 × 208 × 263 mm, holding the cage with the ThinkCentre on top

Take it apart

Here's the whole thing in 3D, built from the same measurements as the printed case. Drag to turn it, tap any part to see what it is, or press Explode to pull the layers apart and follow the cables.

SATA from an M.2 slot

The Tiny has no way to connect four hard drives on its own, so they go through a RIITOP M.2-to-SATA adapter. It fits one of the two M.2 slots on the Tiny's underside, where the NVMe system drive takes the other, and an ASMedia ASM1166 chip turns that slot's two PCIe 3.0 lanes into six SATA ports, four of which run to the drive cage. It's a plain AHCI controller, so Proxmox drives it with the standard Linux driver and ZFS sees each disk directly, with no RAID card in between. That matters, because ZFS wants to manage the disks itself.

Wiring

The drives don't draw power from the ThinkCentre at all. The ESGAMING 300 W is a small Flex-ATX supply, the kind made for 1U servers, and it's fully modular, so only one cable is plugged in: a Molex cable with two plugs that feeds both of the cage's power inputs. That's 12 V for the drive motors and 5 V for their electronics.

A computer's power supply only turns on when the motherboard tells it to, by connecting the green PS_ON wire to ground. There's no motherboard here, so a switch does that job instead: two wires on the supply's 24-pin socket, and nothing else. One catch is that the drives have to be on before the ThinkCentre starts, or ZFS won't find them when it boots.

The drive cage

The Rosewill RSV-SATA-Cage-34 is designed to fill three 5.25-inch bays in a desktop tower, where DVD drives used to go, and turn them into four hot-swap trays for 3.5-inch drives. Each tray has an activity light, a 120 mm fan at the back pulls air over all four drives, and the cage takes its power through two Molex plugs. A failed drive slides out of the front without taking anything apart. With no tower to put it in, the printed case does that job: the cage slides into the bottom and the Tiny sits on ledges above it, with room underneath for the SATA cables.

10 GbE and the fan knob

An Intel X710 network card sits on the ThinkCentre's PCIe riser, so the server has 10 gigabit Ethernet, ten times the speed of its built-in port. Its ports come out of the back next to a little USB fan speed regulator: plugged into a USB port, its knob sets how fast a fan spins.

Why two mirrors?

In each pair, both drives hold the same data, so either one can die without losing anything. ZFS spreads files across the two pairs, so together they behave like one big drive.

  • Mixed sizes are fine. RAIDZ, ZFS's version of RAID 5, would treat all four drives as 2 TB drives and leave about 6 TB usable. As two mirrors, each pair uses its full size: 6 + 2 = 8 TB.
  • Easy upgrades. Swap the two drives in a pair for bigger ones, one at a time, let ZFS copy the data onto each, and the pool grows.
  • Quick rebuilds. Replacing a failed drive only means copying from its twin, which is faster and easier on the other drives than rebuilding RAIDZ.
  • Self-healing. Every block has a checksum. If one copy comes back wrong, ZFS notices and repairs it from the other drive in the pair.

A mirror isn't a backup, though: it protects against a dead drive, not a deleted folder. That's what ZFS snapshots are for.

The network

The firewall runs as a virtual machine on the same Proxmox host. Rather than one flat network where every device can talk to every other device, things are split into segments by how much I trust them:

ServersThe services below. Reachable from home devices, on purpose and only where needed.
IoTSmart plugs, sensors and anything with firmware I don't fully trust. It can reach Home Assistant, not my laptop.
DMZAnything that faces the internet lives here, fenced off from everything else.

For getting in from outside, I use WireGuard: a fast, modern VPN that makes my phone behave as if it were on the home network. Nothing else is exposed directly.

What runs on it

Immich

Our photo and video library, with face recognition and search. Migrated over from a Windows PC. The machine-learning jobs are offloaded to my desktop's RTX 5090, so the little server doesn't break a sweat.

Ente

End-to-end encrypted photo backup. Even the server it runs on can't see the photos.

Samba file share

Plain old network drive for the household's documents, on ZFS so it's snapshotted and checksummed.

Home Assistant

Local home automation that keeps working when the internet doesn't.

WireGuard

Remote access to everything above, from anywhere, without opening services to the internet.

Nextcloud

For sharing files and albums with people outside the house.

Account portal

A small self-service portal I wrote in Rust, so family members can manage their own accounts without asking me.
