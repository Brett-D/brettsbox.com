Why self-host?

Partly for privacy: our family photos are ours, and I'd rather they lived on hardware I can touch. Partly because it's the best way I know to really learn how networks, storage and services fit together. And partly because it's genuinely fun to run your own little corner of the internet.

Inside the box

A ThinkCentre Tiny has no room for 3.5-inch hard drives, so they live in a Rosewill hot-swap cage, made to fill three 5.25-inch bays where a tower PC's DVD drives used to go. With no tower to put it in, a case I designed and printed does that job: the cage slides into the bottom, the ThinkCentre sits on ledges above it, and the drives' own power supply stands behind the cage.

SATA from an M.2 slot

The drives connect through an M.2-to-SATA adapter in the second M.2 slot under the ThinkCentre. Its ASM1166 chip is a plain AHCI controller, so Proxmox uses the standard Linux driver and ZFS sees each disk directly, with no RAID card in between. That matters, because ZFS wants to manage the disks itself.

The drives don't draw power from the ThinkCentre at all. A computer's power supply only turns on when the motherboard tells it to, by connecting the green PS_ON wire to ground. There's no motherboard here, so a switch does that job instead: two wires on the supply's 24-pin socket, and nothing else. One catch is that the drives have to be on before the ThinkCentre starts, or ZFS won't find them when it boots.

Why two mirrors?

In each pair, both drives hold the same data, so either one can die without losing anything. ZFS spreads files across the two pairs, so together they behave like one big drive.

  • Mixed sizes are fine. RAIDZ, ZFS's version of RAID 5, would treat all four drives as 2 TB drives and leave about 6 TB usable. As two mirrors, each pair uses its full size: 6 + 2 = 8 TB.
  • Easy upgrades. Swap the two drives in a pair for bigger ones, one at a time, let ZFS copy the data onto each, and the pool grows.
  • Quick rebuilds. Replacing a failed drive only means copying from its twin, which is faster and easier on the other drives than rebuilding RAIDZ.
  • Self-healing. Every block has a checksum. If one copy comes back wrong, ZFS notices and repairs it from the other drive in the pair.

A mirror isn't a backup, though: it protects against a dead drive, not a deleted folder. That's what ZFS snapshots are for.

The network

The firewall runs as a virtual machine on the same Proxmox host. Rather than one flat network where every device can talk to every other device, things are split into segments by how much I trust them:

ServersThe services below. Reachable from home devices, on purpose and only where needed.
IoTSmart plugs, sensors and anything with firmware I don't fully trust. It can reach Home Assistant, not my laptop.
DMZAnything that faces the internet lives here, fenced off from everything else.

For getting in from outside, I use WireGuard: a fast, modern VPN that makes my phone behave as if it were on the home network. Nothing else is exposed directly.

What runs on it

Immich

Our photo and video library, with face recognition and search. Migrated over from a Windows PC. The machine-learning jobs are offloaded to my desktop's RTX 5090, so the little server doesn't break a sweat.

Ente

End-to-end encrypted photo backup. Even the server it runs on can't see the photos.

Samba file share

Plain old network drive for the household's documents, on ZFS so it's snapshotted and checksummed.

Home Assistant

Local home automation that keeps working when the internet doesn't.

WireGuard

Remote access to everything above, from anywhere, without opening services to the internet.

Nextcloud

For sharing files and albums with people outside the house.

Account portal

A small self-service portal I wrote in Rust, so family members can manage their own accounts without asking me.
